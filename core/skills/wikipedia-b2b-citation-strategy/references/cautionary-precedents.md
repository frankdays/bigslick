# Cautionary Precedents

Real cases worth knowing before approving an execution plan — these are why the disclosure and "one point of contact" rules in SKILL.md are strict rather than aspirational.

## Wiki-PR (2013) — the canonical large-scale failure

Wiki-PR was a consulting firm that marketed the ability to edit Wikipedia by "directly edit[ing] your page using our network of established Wikipedia editors and admins." It received media attention in 2013 after a sockpuppet investigation resulted in more than 250 Wikipedia user accounts being blocked or banned. Confirmed clients included Priceline; the firm claimed to manage Wikipedia presence for more than 12,000 clients.

What specifically went wrong, beyond the account count: in addition to violating rules against sockpuppeting, Wiki-PR violated Wikipedia rules by citing articles that were planted on business content farms and various other websites that accept contributions from any Internet user as sources for Wikipedia entries, creating a false impression of credibility. In other words, the failure compounded across two of the rules in this skill at once — undisclosed coordinated accounts (Track 4) AND unreliable sourcing (Track 2/3) — not just one.

The regulatory consequence that outlasted the company: the Wikimedia Foundation changed its terms of use in the wake of the investigation, requiring anyone paid to edit Wikipedia to openly disclose their affiliations. This is the direct origin of the paid-disclosure legal requirement now baked into the Terms of Use, referenced throughout the policy-foundations file.

Even the ban didn't fully stop it: despite the ban, Status Labs, a firm started in 2012 by Wiki-PR founders... continued to edit clients' Wikipedia articles according to former employees — illustrating that the reputational and operational cost of getting caught doesn't end when the specific accounts are banned.

## Operation Orangemoody (2015) — the extortion variant

A second, related failure mode worth knowing about even though it's not the strategy described in this skill: 2015's Operation Orangemoody uncovered another paid-editing scam, in which 381 accounts were used to extort money from businesses to create and ostensibly protect promotional articles. This is a reminder that the existence of bad-faith "Wikipedia fixer" services in the market is itself a reputational hazard — vetting any agency partner against the disclosure and single-point-of-contact principles in this skill is not optional due diligence.

## BP press office (2013) — the "honest disclosure, still controversial" case

Useful because it shows disclosure alone doesn't eliminate scrutiny — it just keeps the company on the right side of the rules while scrutiny happens: a member of BP's press office had submitted drafts to rewrite the company's article, including sections dealing with its environmental record; the drafts were reviewed and added by other editors. Estimates of the size of the contributions were as high as 44 percent of the article. The press officer had disclosed his affiliation ("Arturo at BP") and had not directly edited the page — he went through the proper talk-page/request channel. Even so, the development caused concern because the content was being produced by an employee, while "readers would be none the wiser" about the scale of that contribution to casual readers.

Takeaway for SKILL.md Track 4: doing it "right" (disclosure, no direct editing) avoids policy violation and the kind of investigation Wiki-PR faced, but a large volume of employee-originated content can still draw scrutiny and press attention on its own. Pace and proportion matter, not just process compliance.

## Leo Burnett Tailor Made — the well-intentioned creative-agency miss

A different failure shape: not paid editing in the financial sense, but still a Terms-of-Use violation born from not understanding the rules: Leo Burnett Tailor Made stated they "found a unique way to contribute photography of adventure destinations to their respective Wikipedia articles while achieving the goal of elevating those images in search rankings" and that they had "since learned that this effort worked counter to Wikipedia's community guidelines." It was described as "wildly misguided" by a University of Pennsylvania marketing professor in New York Times coverage.

Relevant for B2B tech specifically: this is the closest analogue to a tempting B2B move — contributing genuinely useful assets (diagrams, screenshots, data visualizations) to relevant Wikipedia articles with the secondary goal of brand/SEO benefit. The lesson isn't "never contribute media or content" — it's that the *motivation showing through* (optimizing for search ranking rather than encyclopedic value) is what converts a contribution into a violation, even when no money changed hands and nothing was technically hidden.

## The general pattern across all four cases

Every case above fails on one or more of: (1) undisclosed or insufficiently disclosed affiliation, (2) coordinated multi-account activity that creates a false impression of independent consensus, (3) sourcing that doesn't meet the independence bar, or (4) volume/pace disproportionate to a single, transparent point of contact. These map directly onto Track 4 of SKILL.md — which is why that track carries the most prescriptive, least-flexible rules in the whole skill.
