# Wikipedia Policy Foundations

This file holds the actual policy substance behind the strategy in SKILL.md. Read this before executing tactics, not just the summary.

## Notability for organizations (WP:CORP / WP:NCORP)

The core test: a company, corporation, organization, group, product, or service is presumed notable if it has been the subject of significant coverage in multiple reliable secondary sources that are independent of the subject. These criteria, generally, follow the general notability guideline with a stronger emphasis on quality of the sources to prevent gaming of the rules by marketing and public relations professionals.

Notability does not inherit. If a corporation is notable, its subsidiaries do not "inherit" notability from being owned by the corporation — and the reverse is also true: a company isn't notable because a notable customer uses it, or a notable person founded it.

Being a large or publicly traded company is not automatic notability either: there has been considerable discussion over time whether publicly traded corporations... are inherently notable. Consensus has been that notability is not automatic in this (or any other) case. However, sufficient independent sources almost always exist for such companies.

**What doesn't count toward notability**, directly relevant to the "get listed everywhere" instinct: inclusion in collections that have indiscriminate inclusion criteria (i.e. attempt to include every existing item instead of selecting the best, most notable examples), such as databases, archives, directories, dictionaries, bibliographies, certain almanacs... other listings and mentions not accompanied by commentary, survey, study, discussion, analysis, or evaluation of the product, company, or organization.

Practical note on volume: external advisors suggest aiming for at least 5-10 solid, independent references before submitting an article and warn that a single mention in a major publication is not enough... a short quote in an article, a brief feature in a local newspaper, or inclusion in a list of "top companies to watch" doesn't meet the in-depth requirement.

## Conflict of interest and paid editing

The core guideline: if you have a personal connection to a topic or person with an existing article, you are advised to refrain from editing that article directly and to provide full disclosure of the connection if you comment about the article on talk pages or in other discussions.

This is a legal/Terms-of-Use requirement, not just a community norm, for anyone compensated: the Wikimedia Foundation's terms of use require that editors who are being paid for their contributions disclose their employer (the person or organization who is paying for the edits); the client (the person or organization on whose behalf the edits are made); and any other relevant affiliation.

How disclosure must be made — you must disclose your employer, client, and affiliation with respect to any contribution for which you receive, or expect to receive, compensation. You must make that disclosure in at least one of the following ways: a statement on your user page, either custom-written or using {{paid}}, a statement on the talk page accompanying any paid contributions, or a statement in the edit summary accompanying any paid contributions.

What to do instead of direct editing: if there's a mistake in the article: use {{edit COI}} on the talk page... if you want to ask someone to make changes to the article: post requested edits on the article's talk page using {{edit COI}} or at the Conflict of interest noticeboard.

The narrow exception for direct editing: an exception to not editing an article about yourself or someone you know is made if the article contains defamation or a serious error that needs to be corrected quickly.

A practical disclosure template from PR-industry guidance: I am an employee of Acme Technology, and as part of my role, I am working to ensure that Wikipedia articles related to Acme are accurate, neutral, and well-sourced. I understand Wikipedia's policies on COI, neutrality, and verifiability and will not make direct edits to pages related to my employer. Instead, I will suggest changes on article Talk pages for independent editors to review.

This obligation extends to legal compliance beyond Wikipedia's own rules: all editors are expected to follow United States law on undisclosed advertising, which is described by the Federal Trade Commission (FTC)... the FTC regards advertising as deceptive if it mimics a content format, such as a news report, that appears to come from an independent, impartial source.

## Reliable sourcing standards

The bar: articles should be based on reliable, independent, published sources with a reputation for fact-checking and accuracy.

Press releases specifically: a press release is clearly not an independent source as it is usually written either by the business or organization it is written about, or by a business or person hired by or affiliated with the organization. A secondary analysis adds: press releases are not allowed as reliable sources on Wikipedia. Whether issued by a corporation, government body, nonprofit, or individual, a press release is considered a primary source produced by an interested party, which makes it inherently promotional and editorially unreliable by Wikipedia's standards.

This extends to press releases laundered through wire distribution: any article citing PR Newswire, VerticalNews or similar online business news sources should be considered a primary source unless there is evidence — not in the byline but the body of the article — of independent authorship and editorial review.

On sponsored or "contributor" content specifically: sponsored content is a paid advertisement that is formatted to look like an article or other piece of typical content for that outlet... advertisements can be cited, but they are non-independent and should be treated as self-published and primary sources. A secondary source warns specifically about platforms relevant to B2B/tech: a great example is Forbes and Entrepreneur. Two great publications that each have Wikipedia pages of their own. Unfortunately, each article cited from each needs to be given consideration to who wrote it... both allow contributors to submit content... the issue is that both sites relax editorial guidelines for contributors.

What independence actually means in practice, including the key distinction between attributed and unattributed claims: "Organization X said 10,000 people showed up to the protest they organized" is usually acceptable when relying on material published by the organization, but "10,000 people showed up to protest" is usually not, as the organizers of the event have an incentive to exaggerate. But uncontroversial self-sourced facts are fine: uncontroversial statements for which the information logically comes from the subject, such as "Big Company has 15,000 employees", does not require WP:INTEXT attribution.

## Wikidata: the lower-bar alternative

Notability on Wikidata is structurally different and easier to clear: Wikidata's inclusion criterion is simpler: the entity must be real (not fictional), have at least one external identifier or reference that makes it verifiable, and not be a duplicate of an existing item.

What counts as that reference: if your brand exists in Crunchbase, an industry database, a government business registry, an authoritative directory, or any similar structured source, that external reference can be enough to justify a Wikidata entry... the bar is "verifiable existence" rather than "fame."

What gets entries deleted: no external references. An entry that only links to the brand's own website fails Wikidata's verifiability requirement... promotional tone in the description... duplicate entries. Creating a new entry when one already exists is the most common reason for deletion.

Realistic timeline and payoff: a basic entry can be live within a day of submission. The full structured entry with proper properties, references, and connections to related entities is usually a few weeks of incremental work. One practitioner's reported outcome: the two hours a client invested in creating their Wikidata entry produced a Knowledge Panel appearance within six weeks and noticeably more accurate ChatGPT descriptions of their services within three months.

## Sockpuppetry and meatpuppetry — why "one disclosed point of contact" matters

The policy distinction that matters most for a company running coordinated outreach: sometimes users who appear to work with a common agenda are not sockpuppets (one user, multiple accounts), but multiple users editing with the sole purpose of backing each other up, often called "meatpuppets"... for the purposes of upholding policy, Wikipedia does not distinguish between meatpuppets and sockpuppets. This means even fully honest, individually-disclosed employees coordinating on the same pages can trigger the same investigation and consequences as fraudulent sockpuppets.

What specifically gets flagged: advertising Wikipedia articles to your friends, family members, or communities of people who agree with you, so that they come to Wikipedia and support your side of a debate and give the appearance of consensus is strongly discouraged.

PR-industry guidance converges on the same operational rule: don't overman your Wikipedia effort. Limit the number of people a Wikipedian speaks to. Having several points of contact for a Wikipedian could be irritating or confusing. Where possible, we suggest restricting the number of people per organisation that maintain a relationship with a Wikipedian. And on the underlying logic of why coordination backfires even when honest: where you are trying to get a consensus on changes, it is not going to help to say your colleague agrees with you!
